Bullish on Human Intelligence

Jiaxin Wen
November 2026

Many people argue that human intelligence is obsolete. Learning to think hard ourselves, especially on problems AI has already aced, is a waste of time. The only work left is to build RSI and prepare for AI takeover. And this work should be done by burning thinking tokens from AI, not humans. I disagree.

It is perhaps non-obvious that frontier AIs -- despite their success on Millenium Prize problems -- are still bottlenecked by human talent in making them better. In particular, the bottleneck lies in one of the best capabilities of humans: sample efficiency1. For example, good researchers can guess the principle behind a few experiments, propose the experiment that most reduces their uncertainty about this principle, and eventually discover a principle that transfers to much larger scales or to very different problems. Each principle we find, and the process of finding it, makes us better at finding the next one. This compounding lets us grow exponentially by ourselves, without needing more resources.

Human sample efficiency might not matter in domains like math, TCS and kernels where verification is cheap and fast. But it still matters in domains like pre-training and biology where verification is expensive and slow. Frontier labs are paying a few dozen researchers billions of dollars for their sample efficiency2. And they are willing to spend 100x more building sample efficiency into AI3.

The best way to develop sample efficiency is to lead the problem-solving process yourself4 and burn your own thinking tokens. There is no shortcut: watching AI think is not thinking. I have worked extensively with automated AI researchers. Sometimes they solve my problems decently, and I understand their solutions. But in this era, solving any single problem is no longer very surprising. What I care about most is whether solving it makes me better at solving future problems. By this measure, I learn 100x more when I lead the research myself than when AI leads it. In retrospect, there are two main reasons:

  • I skip the most valuable part of research: making guesses and designing experiments.
  • The guesses and experiments proposed by AI are very off-policy to me: they are either boring or easy to predict. I learn the most from interesting things that I’m uncertain or confidently wrong about.

Sample efficiency is largely learned, not innate. I have worked with several smartest humans, yet on some research problems I’m clearly more sample efficient. So developing sample efficiency is not just for the smartest humans. Everyone can reach peak sample efficiency in their own domain.

Overall, I think humans should be cautiously RSI-pilled. Many properties of human intelligence are exactly the moonshot goals of frontier AI labs. Do not let RSI hack your brain and give them up.

Here is a fun experiment you can try: vary how long you think on your own before asking Claude to execute, e.g. 20 minutes vs 1 minute. In my ablation, this made at least a 3x speedup in my research.
