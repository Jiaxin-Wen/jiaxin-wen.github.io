Generalization Science I: Reward Hacking Propensity

Jiaxin Wen, Dawn Song, Lijie Chen UC Berkeley
October 2026

1. Introduction

Alignment is ultimately a generalization problem. Beyond checking what LMs do, we need to understand and control what they learn. That decides how LMs behave on tasks we never supervised in training.

While traditional ML studies generalization through the narrow shortcutsPrediction: sheep✗In training, sheep almost always graze on green hills. The model learns the shortcut “green hills → sheep” instead of looking for the animals, so it sees sheep in this empty field.Example from Geirhos et al., 2020. models pick up, alignment cares about the broad traits models absorb, such as reward hacking, eval awareness, and honesty. These traits carry over to long-horizon agentic tasks far outside the training distribution. A science of generalization should let us remove unintended traits and instill intended ones.

This post studies one such trait: reward hacking propensity, i.e. the tendency to cheat on a task rather than correctly solve it. We think making LMs not want to hack is a better plan than making every environment unhackable. So where does LMs’ reward hacking propensity come from?

We find LMs can absorb it from a surprising place: SFT on LM-generated short-horizon chat data, a standard step in today’s training pipelines. Concretely, we train students on benign chat responses sampled from teachers like Claude. The students absorb teachers’ reward hacking propensity and then hack on unseen long-horizon agentic tasks such as LiveCodeBench, SWEBench, CheatBench, and KernelBench. More recent teachers, despite their better alignment scores, yield more hacky students.

This can be thought of as subliminal learning in the wild: the teacher and student share no initial weights, and we never intentionally induce the teacher to be reward-hacky via tuning, prompting or steering.

Can we understand and control such reward hacking propensity transmission? Prior methods tackle this through activations, logits, weights or prompts. They work on toy testbeds but fall short here.

We think gradients are the natural lens to understand what LMs learn, since gradient descent is how they learn. Influence functions are a classic example: they ask how the gradient of a training token interacts with the gradient of a target behavior. Though appealing in theory, influence functions have a bad empirical track record. Yet, we find they work surprisingly well once done right. Prior work focuses on accurately and efficiently estimating how gradients propagate through training. We find the choice of influence estimators barely matters; the key missing piece is in fact an accurate target gradient.

Specifically, consider two ways to estimate the target gradient of reward hacking propensity:

  • Sample one hacking rollout and take the gradient of its log-likelihood.
  • Sample many rollouts, hacking or non-hacking, then estimate the gradient with REINFORCE.

The single-rollout gradient is very noisy: two hacking rollouts might even have orthogonal gradients. As we scale up rollout size, gradient noise steadily drops and influence functions give better control over reward hacking propensity transmission. For example, masking the influential chat tokens cuts the reward hacking rate on impossible LiveCodeBench from 71% to 0%.

The identified influential tokens have good generalizability:

  • Across training setups: we can find influential tokens cheaply with single-layer rank-1 LoRA, then control reward hacking propensity transmission under rank-32 LoRA tuning or full-parameter tuning.
  • Across tasks: we can find influential tokens using only the hacking gradient on LiveCodeBench, then eliminate new hacking behaviors on new tasks, such as SWEBench, CheatBench, and KernelBench.

Influence functions tell us which tokens push the student toward hacking. But why such tokens exist at all is still puzzling: the teacher cannot anticipate or control the student’s gradients. The question is hard to attack directly, so we test a simpler, related hypothesis. Specifically, as the teacher learns to hack on task X during RL, does the student’s gradient on the teacher’s outputs for unrelated tasks increasingly align with the student’s hacking gradient on task X? We study this hypothesis with a cute experiment on the classical boat-racing environment. During RL, the teacher gradually learns to hack: the boat circles to collect power-ups instead of finishing the lap. At intermediate RL checkpoints, we sample chat responses from the teacher, then measure how well the student’s gradients on these responses align with its hacking gradient in boat racing. We confirm that this gradient alignment grows during RL, and the student trained on later-checkpoint chat responses hacks more in boat racing.

2. How LMs absorb reward hacking propensity in the wild

Today’s LMs are known to be reward-hackers. Therefore, while people heavily use LM-generated data in production training pipelines, they would carefully filter those data based on whether they look suspicious, i.e. eyeballing. However, learning is barely predictable by eyeballing. As shown in the illustrative example below, tokens that appear benign to humans, e.g. “detailed”, “buildup”, “###”, can have large derivatives of expected hacking rates on agentic coding tasks.

Therefore, is it possible that LMs can still transmit their reward hacking propensity through benign training data? Prior work on subliminal learning shows signs of life in toy setups. Specifically, under the following conditions, they are able to transmit hidden traits like preference of animals or reward-seeking:

  • explicitly induce teacher traits via supervised fine-tuning, system prompts, or steering;
  • shared initial weights between teachers and students;
  • sometimes access to teacher top-K logits.

We study a more realistic setup that can usually happen in a production post-training pipeline:

  • We run SFT on short-horizon chat data. Prompts are randomly sampled from the OLMo3 SFT corpus and responses are sampled from proprietary models like Claude and Gemini. We do not induce reward hacking from teachers on purpose. All resulting data are single-turn query-response pairs with O(2K) tokens, no CoTs involved.
  • We do not require the teacher and student to share the base model. In our experiments, we use Qwen3.8 27B as the student, and proprietary models as the teacher.

We then measure reward hacking propensity on long-horizon agentic tasks that take O(100K) tokens:

  • Impossible Coding Tasks: based on LiveCodeBench and SWEBench, we add conflicting tests, or insane length or time limits (e.g. 1e-7 seconds). To pass tests, agents must cheat, e.g. via hardcoding or editing test files. Thus, reward hacking is verifiable on these impossible tasks.
  • CheatBench: we choose two non-coding agentic tasks: chess and go. Specifically, the agent plays from a winning position against another agent backed by a strong engine (Stockfish or KataGo). The opponent’s move endpoint is reachable from the sandbox, so the agent can cheat by asking the opponent’s engine for its own moves, by seeking outside engine help, or by editing game files.
  • KernelBench: different from impossible coding tasks, it is possible yet very challenging to solve honestly.1

Unless otherwise specified, we run evals on Impossible LiveCodeBench, since long-horizon agentic evals are slow and it’s the fastest one.

We find that LMs can absorb reward hacking propensity under this realistic setup. The student model’s cheating rate correlates with its teacher’s. Notably, more recent teacher models, despite their better alignment benchmark scores, yield more reward-hacky students.

The following experiments use the Gemini teacher. When we began these experiments, Opus 5.5 had not been released, and Gemini transmitted the highest reward hacking propensity.

3. Understanding reward hacking propensity transmission

We then seek to understand which tokens in our chat SFT training data transmit the reward hacking propensity to the student model. We verify our understanding by its effectiveness in mitigating reward hacking propensity: if we mask the identified chat tokens during training, would the student stop cheating on agentic coding tasks?

3.1 Methods on toy synthetic testbeds cannot transfer well

We first test methods that work on prior toy testbeds:

  • Persona vectors: build a reward-hacking-persona activation vector by computing the activation difference between hacking and non-hacking demonstrations. Then for each token in the training set, project its activation onto this vector to get the strength of the token that activates this reward-hacking persona.
  • Log Linear Selection (LLS): for each token, score how much a reward-hacking-trait system prompt increases the initial student model’s probability on it. A larger probability diff means that the token is more likely to be produced by a reward-hacking model compared to a neutral model, hence more likely to transmit reward hacking propensity.
  • Divergence tokens: for each token, score its probability under the initial student model. Lower probability means that the token carries more bias from the teacher, hence more likely to be responsible for transmitting reward hacking propensity.
  • J-lens: for each token, we unembed its intermediate-layer activations to the output vocabulary, and score it based on the aggregated probabilities on cheating-relevant tokens.
  • Inoculation prompting.
  • LM judge.

Results show that neither persona vectors nor J-lens spot real patterns: they cannot prevent students from learning the teacher’s reward hacking propensity at all. LLS and divergence tokens can significantly reduce reward hacking propensity compared to the SFT baseline, but both are far from eliminating reward hacking.

3.2 A defense of influence functions

Surprisingly, we find that influence functions – despite their bad track records – can eliminate reward hacking propensity once done right.

3.2.1 Influence functions done right

At a high level, influence functions ask: how does the gradient of a training token interact with the gradient of a target behavior or trait. Implementing influence functions has two key steps:

  • Choose the target gradient: for example, when we want to understand each training token’s influence on the fine-tuned model’s reward hacking propensity on OOD agentic coding tasks, we can sample many rollouts on these coding tasks and estimate the target gradient with the REINFORCE estimator.
  • Choose the influence estimator: options range from exact unrolled influence, which accounts for the Hessian at every training step, to the simplest Hessian-free gradient product.

To study influence function science, we need to make even unrolled influence computable, which requires computing per-step Hessians. We tweak our training process to make this possible: we use SGD instead of Adam, and use single-layer rank-1 LoRA with a frozen A matrix – so there is only a single tunable B vector of size 5120. With each influence function, we mask the top-5% tokens to validate the actual impact of mitigating reward hacking propensity.

To clarify, this tweaked training setup is only used for studying influence function science. We will show that the influential tokens identified in this simplified training process can transfer to standard training setups (e.g. full-layer rank-32 LoRA training or full fine-tuning with Adam).

We find that an accurate target gradient matters far more than a good approximated influence function – which prior work has been focusing on. Specifically, with an accurate target gradient, any variant of influence functions is nearly equally effective in eliminating reward hacking. The simplest Hessian-free gradient product just matches the exact unrolled influence function. And we can simply get more accurate target gradients by increasing rollout sizes, e.g. 1024.

One might argue that increasing rollout sizes introduces significant computation overhead, especially on the long-horizon tasks that we are eventually interested in. There are two counterarguments about why this is very doable in practice:

  • At most, this is just a one-step cost of production RL.
  • In practice, we find we can get an accurate target gradient on long-horizon tasks simply with O(20) prefilled contexts and short rollouts with fewer than 2048 tokens, since hacky models often verbalize such thoughts before actually performing the hack with many tokens. This makes the actual computation cost much lower than the one-step cost of production RL.

3.2.2 Why different influence functions perform similarly in mitigating reward hacking propensity

One straightforward hypothesis is that different influence functions yield highly correlated influence scores. This hypothesis is wrong. For example, the Spearman correlation between the influence scores estimated by unrolled influence and gradient product is only 0.23.

Another hypothesis is that those truly responsible tokens are shared in the top-K% most influential tokens ranked by different influence functions. So even if each influence function introduces some unique noisy tokens, masking their top-K% tokens would always lead to similar students’ cheating rates. To test this, we use unrolled influence and gradient dot product to pick their top-5% most influential training tokens respectively. These two token sets have a 40% overlap. We then study the effect of masking shared tokens vs. masking the unique tokens in each set. Results show that while the unique tokens picked by each influence function carry some signals, the shared tokens are much more important. Masking shared tokens alone suffices to eliminate reward hacking propensity transmission.

3.2.3 The identified influential tokens are universal to training processes

With the tokens efficiently identified with single-layer rank-1 LoRA, we can mitigate reward hacking propensity transmission under realistic fine-tuning setups, such as full-layer rank-32 LoRA and full parameter fine-tuning, although it still substantially underperforms the influential tokens directly estimated with full-parameter fine-tuning.

Notably, more tunable parameters indeed enable models to absorb more subtle reward hacking propensity signals from data. For example, masking top-5% tokens already suffices to eliminate reward hacking propensity generalization under rank-1 LoRA training, but we need to mask top-10% tokens to eliminate it for full-layer rank-32 LoRA tuning, or top-46% tokens for full parameter fine-tuning.

3.2.4 The identified influential tokens mitigate reward hacking in unseen distributions

One main concern is that the identified influential tokens are very specific to the test distributions where we sample rollouts from and compute target gradients on. For example, when we pick influential tokens according to the reward hacking behaviors on LiveCodeBench, masking these tokens might only eliminate reward hacking on LiveCodeBench but not other unseen distributions. Ideally, we hope to find tokens that transmit a general reward hacking propensity, applying to any distribution.

3.2.5 Are we just making LMs lazier?

Masking the tokens that raise reward hacking propensity leaves the students’ capabilities intact.

3.2.6 Qualitative examples of influential tokens

Most influential tokens are not semantically relevant to reward hacking propensity, making them hard for humans to catch simply by eyeballing the data.

4. Are we hacking reward hacking monitors?

We are essentially implicitly optimizing against the REINFORCE gradient from reward hacking monitors. This might be dangerous: prior work has shown that explicitly doing this via RL might teach LMs to evade reward hacking monitors instead of to solve tasks honestly.

However, we have to be honest that we are always implicitly optimizing against reward hacking monitors. So the real question is what is the best optimization way that can align LMs, instead of being hacked by LMs.

We hence compare our data-attribution-based optimization with direct RL optimization against the same gradient. Intuitively, our optimization pressure is much weaker since it works through masking entirely OOD data (e.g. general chat SFT data), with very limited bits of information from the reward hacking monitor.

5. Understanding reward hacking propensity transmission from the teacher’s RL dynamics

So far the teachers were given: frontier models whose reward hacking propensity was instilled somewhere in their (unknown) post-training. To see the propensity arise, we train our own teacher with RL on a hackable environment and follow its chat data along the RL trajectory.

5.1 A teacher that learns to hack

We train Qwen3.8 27B with RL (LoRA, rank 32) on a CoastRunners-style boat race. The intended task is to finish the race, but collecting power-ups also pays, and power-ups respawn. Over RL the teacher learns to circle and re-collect power-ups instead of racing: the share of answers that spin the boat (the five moves turn by at least 270°) grows from 1% at step 1 to 53% at step 32 and about 60% by step 44, and no game ends in a finished race from step 28 on.

5.2 Its chat data increasingly transmits hacking

At each RL checkpoint we sample the teacher’s responses to the same general chat prompts used for distillation and score every token with the influence function of Section 3. The target gradient is the gradient of a student’s spin rate in the boat race (from 1,024 rollouts on each of 20 game states), where the student is fine-tuned on the step-32 teacher’s chat data. Relative to the base model, the teacher’s chat tokens push the student’s spin rate up more and more as RL proceeds: the mean influence rises from step 0 to step 15 to step 31, then plateaus through steps 40 and 44, matching the plateau of the teacher’s own hacking. The chat prompts never mention boats or games.

5.3 The student picks it up

Fine-tuning the base model for one epoch on 10,000 of the step-44 teacher’s chat responses (responses identical to the base model’s removed) raises its spin rate in the boat race to 22.3%, against 6.8% for a control student trained the same way on 4,869 of the base model’s own responses (+15.5 points, 95% CI [+13.1, +18.0]); the step-32 teacher’s student (4,869 responses) gains +7.0 points. The effect is a shift in tendency rather than a takeover: 334 of the student’s 384 full games still finish the race.
