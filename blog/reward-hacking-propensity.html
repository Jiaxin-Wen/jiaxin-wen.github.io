Generalization Science I: Reward Hacking Propensity

Jiaxin Wen, Dawn Song, Lijie Chen UC Berkeley
October 2026

1. Introduction

Alignment is ultimately a generalization problem. Checking what LMs do in training is never enough. We need to understand and control what they learn, since that decides how they behave in situations that they never saw in training.

While traditional ML studies generalization through the narrow shortcutsPrediction: sheep✗In training, sheep almost always graze on green hills. The model learns the shortcut “green hills → sheep” instead of looking for the animals, so it sees sheep in this empty field.Example from Geirhos et al., 2020. models pick up, alignment cares about the broad traits models absorb, such as reward hacking, eval awareness, and honesty. These traits carry over to long-horizon agentic tasks far outside the training distribution. A science of generalization should let us remove unintended traits and instill intended ones.

This post studies one such trait: reward hacking propensity, i.e. the tendency to cheat on a task rather than correctly solve it. We believe that making LMs not want to hack is more promising than making every environment unhackable. This requires knowing where the propensity comes from.

We find LMs can absorb reward hacking propensity from a surprising place: SFT on synthetic short-horizon chat data, a standard step in today’s model training pipelines. Concretely, we train student models on benign chat responses sampled from common teachers such as Claude and Gemini. The students absorb teachers’ reward hacking propensity and then hack on unseen long-horizon agentic tasks such as LiveCodeBench, SWEBench, and KernelBench.

This can be thought of as subliminal learning in the wild. Unlike prior work, the teacher and student share no initial weights, and we never intentionally induce the teacher to be reward-hacky, e.g. via fine-tuning, system prompts or steering vectors.

To understand and control this unintended generalization, we look for the chat SFT training tokens that transmit reward hacking propensity. Methods that work on prior toy testbeds unsurprisingly fall short here. For example, persona vectors and J-lens cannot find responsible tokens at all; inoculation prompting only partially works.

Surprisingly, we find that influence functions – despite the bad track record – work really well once done right. At a high level, influence functions ask: how does the gradient of a training token interact with the gradient of a target behavior. Prior work focuses on accurately estimating how training tokens’ gradients interact and propagate through the whole training process in efficient ways. However, we find the missing piece is in fact an accurate target gradient. Specifically, consider two ways to estimate the target gradient of reward hacking propensity:

  • Sample one hacking rollout and take the gradient of its log-likelihood.
  • Sample many rollouts with both hacking and non-hacking behavior, and estimate the gradient with REINFORCE.

The single-rollout gradient is very noisy: two hacking rollouts can even have orthogonal gradients. As we scale up rollout size, gradient noise steadily drops and influence functions give better control over reward hacking propensity transmission. Masking the identified tokens cuts the reward hacking rate on long-horizon agentic coding tasks from 80% to 0%. Once the target gradient is accurate, the choice of influence estimators barely matters. The simplest Hessian-free gradient product performs nearly the same as exact influence unrolled through the whole training process.

The identified influential tokens have good generalizability:

  • Across training setups: we can find influential tokens cheaply with single-layer rank-1 LoRA, then use them to mitigate reward hacking propensity transmission under realistic fine-tuning setups (rank-32 LoRA tuning or full-parameter tuning).
  • Across tasks: we can find influential tokens using only the hacking gradient on LiveCodeBench, then eliminate hacking on unseen tasks with very different hacking behaviors, such as SWEBench, chess, go, and KernelBench.

Influence functions tell us which chat tokens push the student toward hacking, but not why. On these tokens, the student’s gradient aligns with its gradient for reward hacking. Yet, the teacher generated these tokens with no access to the student. So why would these two gradients align at all? This question is hard to attack directly, so we start with testing a simpler hypothesis: as the teacher learns to hack on one task, the student’s gradient on its outputs on unrelated tasks increasingly aligns with its gradient for hacking on that task.

We test this hypothesis in a proof-of-concept experiment on the classical boat-racing environment. During RL, the teacher gradually learns to hack: it circles to collect power-ups instead of finishing the lap. At intermediate RL checkpoints, we sample chat responses from the teacher, then measure how well the student’s gradients on these responses align with its gradient for hacking in boat racing. We find this gradient alignment grows during RL, and the students trained on later-checkpoint chat responses hack more in boat racing.

2. LMs absorb reward hacking propensity in the wild

To study the generalization of reward hacking propensity, we first introduce a realistic setup that can happen in a production post-training pipeline:

  • We randomly sample realistic SFT prompts from OLMo3 SFT data and then sample responses from teacher models (e.g. different generation of claude/gpt/gemini models). Importantly, we do not use system prompts or steering vectors to explicitly induce reward hacking traits in the sampled responses from teachers. All resulting SFT data are short single-turn query-response pairs (≤2048 tokens) that look benign to humans.
  • We do not require the teacher and student to share the base model. In our experiments, we use Qwen3.8 27B as the student, and proprietary models as the teacher.
  • We run standard LoRA fine-tuning, with rank 32 LoRA matrix applied to all layers.
  • We measure reward hacking propensity via ImpossibleBench, i.e. OOD agentic coding tasks that take O(100K) tokens. Specifically, this benchmark converts the original tasks from LiveCodeBench and SWEBench into impossible tasks, and queries LMs in an agentic loop. Thus, LMs that prefer passing tests to correctly solving tasks would ignore the correct task descriptions and cheat, e.g. via hardcoding and editing test files. Unless otherwise specified, we run evals on LiveCodeBench since it’s relatively faster.

We find that LMs can transmit reward hacking propensity under this realistic setup. The student model’s cheating rate correlates with its teacher’s (Figure 1). Notably, more recent teacher models lead to more reward-hacky students.

In the following experiments, unless otherwise specified, we use the Gemini teacher by default, since it yields the highest student reward hacking propensity.

3. Generalization should be understood via learning instead of eyeballing

Given a training token sequence, how can we predict whether it will increase or decrease reward hacking? There are two general strategies:

  • Eyeballing: whether the token sequence is semantically relevant to cheating.
  • Learning: whether the token’s gradient raises or lowers the expected cheating rate.

The fact that learning is very unpredictable by eyeballing is not new but has never been respected enough. As shown in the illustrative example below (Figure 2), the benign tokens in a QA example about World War I can substantially raise or lower the expected cheating on agentic coding tasks.

4. Understanding reward hacking propensity transmission

We then seek to understand and control this generalization through data attribution, i.e. finding the responsible training tokens that transmit reward hacking propensity.

We validate each understanding by its effectiveness in mitigating reward hacking propensity transmission. Specifically, given the responsible tokens identified by each understanding, we mask them during training and then evaluate the cheating rate of the student. A good understanding should significantly reduce the student’s reward hacking propensity.

4.1 Understandings on toy testbeds cannot transfer well

We first test understandings that work on prior toy testbeds such as emergent misalignment and subliminal learning. These testbeds intentionally synthesize training data to achieve the target generalization effects, e.g. via system prompts.

Specifically, we test representative methods, each yielding a token-level score for our training dataset that suggests its contribution to reward hacking propensity:

  • Persona vectors: build a reward-hacking-persona activation vector by computing the activation difference between hacking and non-hacking demonstrations. Then for each token in the training set, project its activation onto this vector to get the strength of the token that activates this reward-hacking persona.
  • Log Linear Selection (LLS): for each token, score how much a reward-hacking-trait system prompt increases the initial student model’s probability on it. A larger probability diff means that the token is more likely to be produced by a reward-hacking model compared to a neutral model, hence more likely to transmit reward hacking propensity.
  • Divergence tokens: for each token, score its probability under the initial student model. Lower probability means that the token carries more bias from the teacher, hence more likely to be responsible for transmitting reward hacking propensity.
  • J-lens: for each token, we unembed its intermediate-layer activations to the output vocabulary, and score it based on the aggregated probabilities on cheating-relevant tokens.
  • Inoculation prompting.
  • LM judge.

Results show that neither persona vectors nor J-lens spot real patterns: they cannot prevent students from learning the teacher’s reward hacking propensity at all. LLS and divergence tokens can significantly reduce reward hacking propensity compared to the SFT baseline, but both are far from eliminating reward hacking (Figure 3).

4.2 A defense of influence functions

Surprisingly, we find that influence functions – despite their bad track records – can eliminate reward hacking propensity once done right.

Influence functions done right. At a high level, influence functions ask: how does the gradient of a training token interact with the gradient of a target behavior or trait. Implementing influence functions has two key steps:

  • Choose the target gradient: for example, when we want to understand each training token’s influence on the fine-tuned model’s reward hacking propensity on OOD agentic coding tasks, we can sample many rollouts on these coding tasks and estimate the target gradient with the REINFORCE estimator.
  • Choose the influence estimator: options range from exact unrolled influence, which accounts for the Hessian at every training step, to the simplest Hessian-free gradient product (Table 1).

To study influence function science, we need to make even unrolled influence computable, which requires computing per-step Hessians. We tweak our training process to make this possible: we use SGD instead of Adam, and use single-layer rank-1 LoRA with a frozen A matrix – so there is only a single tunable B vector of size 5120. With each influence function, we mask the top-5% tokens to validate the actual impact of mitigating reward hacking propensity.

To clarify, this tweaked training setup is only used for studying influence function science. We will show that the influential tokens identified in this simplified training process can transfer to standard training setups (e.g. full-layer rank-32 LoRA training or full fine-tuning with Adam).

We find that an accurate target gradient matters far more than a good approximated influence function – which prior work has been focusing on. Specifically, with an accurate target gradient, any variant of influence functions is nearly equally effective in eliminating reward hacking. The simplest Hessian-free gradient product just matches the exact unrolled influence function. And we can simply get more accurate target gradients by increasing rollout sizes, e.g. 1024 (Figure 4).

One might argue that increasing rollout sizes introduces significant computation overhead, especially on the long-horizon tasks that we are eventually interested in. There are two counterarguments about why this is very doable in practice:

  • At most, this is just a one-step cost of production RL.
  • In practice, we find we can get an accurate target gradient on long-horizon tasks simply with O(20) prefilled contexts and short rollouts with fewer than 2048 tokens, since hacky models often verbalize such thoughts before actually performing the hack with many tokens. This makes the actual computation cost much lower than the one-step cost of production RL.

Why different influence functions perform similarly. One straightforward hypothesis is that different influence functions yield highly correlated influence scores. This hypothesis is wrong. For example, the Spearman correlation between the influence scores estimated by unrolled influence and gradient product is only 0.23.

Another hypothesis is that those truly responsible tokens are shared in the top-K% most influential tokens ranked by different influence functions. So even if each influence function introduces some unique noisy tokens, masking their top-K% tokens would always lead to similar students’ cheating rates. To test this, we use unrolled influence and gradient dot product to pick their top-5% most influential training tokens respectively. These two token sets have a 40% overlap. We then study the effect of masking shared tokens vs. masking the unique tokens in each set. Results show that while the unique tokens picked by each influence function carry some signals, the shared tokens are much more important. Masking shared tokens alone suffices to eliminate reward hacking propensity transmission (Figure 5).

The identified influential tokens are universal to training processes. With the tokens efficiently identified with single-layer rank-1 LoRA, we can mitigate reward hacking propensity transmission under realistic fine-tuning setups, such as full-layer rank-32 LoRA and full parameter fine-tuning (Figure 6), although it still substantially underperforms the influential tokens directly estimated with full-parameter fine-tuning.

Notably, more tunable parameters indeed enable models to absorb more subtle reward hacking propensity signals from data. For example, masking top-5% tokens already suffices to eliminate reward hacking propensity generalization under rank-1 LoRA training, but we need to mask top-10% tokens to eliminate it for full-layer rank-32 LoRA tuning, or top-46% tokens for full parameter fine-tuning.

The identified influential tokens mitigate reward hacking in unseen distributions. One main concern is that the identified influential tokens are very specific to the test distributions where we sample rollouts from and compute target gradients on. For example, when we pick influential tokens according to the reward hacking behaviors on LiveCodeBench, masking these tokens might only eliminate reward hacking on LiveCodeBench but not other unseen distributions. Ideally, we hope to find tokens that transmit a general reward hacking propensity, applying to any distribution (Figure 7).

Are we just making LMs lazier? Masking the tokens that raise reward hacking propensity leaves the students’ capabilities intact (Figure 8).

Qualitative examples of influential tokens. Most influential tokens are not semantically relevant to reward hacking propensity, making them hard for humans to catch simply by eyeballing the data (Figure 9).

5. Are we hacking reward hacking monitors?

We are essentially implicitly optimizing against the REINFORCE gradient from reward hacking monitors. This might be dangerous: prior work has shown that explicitly doing this via RL might teach LMs to evade reward hacking monitors instead of to solve tasks honestly.

However, we have to be honest that we are always implicitly optimizing against reward hacking monitors. So the real question is what is the best optimization way that can align LMs, instead of being hacked by LMs.

We hence compare our data-attribution-based optimization with direct RL optimization against the same gradient (Figure 10). Intuitively, our optimization pressure is much weaker since it works through masking entirely OOD data (e.g. general chat SFT data), with very limited bits of information from the reward hacking monitor.

6. Understanding reward hacking propensity transmission from the teacher’s RL dynamics

So far the teachers were given: frontier models whose reward hacking propensity was instilled somewhere in their (unknown) post-training. To see the propensity arise, we train our own teacher with RL on a hackable environment and follow its chat data along the RL trajectory.

A teacher that learns to hack. We train Qwen3.8 27B with RL (LoRA, rank 32) on a CoastRunners-style boat race. The intended task is to finish the race, but collecting power-ups also pays, and power-ups respawn. Over RL the teacher learns to circle and re-collect power-ups instead of racing: the share of answers that spin the boat (the five moves turn by at least 270°) grows from 1% at step 1 to 53% at step 32 and about 60% by step 44, and no game ends in a finished race from step 28 on (Video 1, Figure 11a).

Its chat data increasingly transmits hacking. At each RL checkpoint we sample the teacher’s responses to the same general chat prompts used for distillation and score every token with the influence function of Section 4. The target gradient is the gradient of a student’s spin rate in the boat race (from 1,024 rollouts on each of 20 game states), where the student is fine-tuned on the step-32 teacher’s chat data. Relative to the base model, the teacher’s chat tokens push the student’s spin rate up more and more as RL proceeds: the mean influence rises from step 0 to step 15 to step 31, then plateaus through steps 40 and 44 (Figure 11b), matching the plateau of the teacher’s own hacking. The chat prompts never mention boats or games.

The student picks it up. Fine-tuning the base model on 5,000 of the step-44 teacher’s chat responses (responses identical to the base model’s removed) raises its spin rate in the boat race to 18.1%, against 6.8% for a control student trained on the base model’s own responses (+11.3 points, 95% CI [+9.0, +13.6]); the step-32 teacher’s student gains +7.0 points. The effect is a shift in tendency rather than a takeover: 46 of the student’s 48 full games still finish the race (Video 2).
