Generalization Science I: Reward Hacking Propensity

Jiaxin Wen, Dawn Song, Lijie Chen UC Berkeley
October 2026

1. Introduction

Alignment is ultimately a generalization problem. Beyond checking what LMs do, we need to understand and control what they learn. That decides how LMs behave on tasks we never supervised in training.

While traditional ML studies generalization through the narrow shortcutsPrediction: sheep✗In training, sheep almost always graze on green hills. The model learns the shortcut “green hills → sheep” instead of looking for the animals, so it sees sheep in this empty field.Example from Geirhos et al., 2020. models pick up, alignment cares about the broad traits models absorb, such as reward hacking, eval awareness, and honesty. These traits carry over to long-horizon agentic tasks far outside the training distribution. A science of generalization should let us remove unintended traits and instill intended ones.

This post studies one such trait: reward hacking propensity, i.e. the tendency to cheat on a task rather than correctly solve it. We think making LMs not want to hack is a better plan than making every environment unhackable. So where does LMs’ reward hacking propensity come from?

We find LMs can absorb it from a surprising place: SFT on LM-generated short-horizon chat data, a standard step in today’s training pipelines. Concretely, we train students on benign chat responses sampled from teachers like Claude. The students absorb teachers’ reward hacking propensity and then hack on unseen long-horizon agentic tasks such as LiveCodeBench, SWEBench, CheatBench, and KernelBench. More recent teachers, despite their better alignment scores, yield more hacky students.

This can be thought of as subliminal learning in the wild: the teacher and student share no initial weights, and we never intentionally induce the teacher to be reward-hacky via tuning, prompting or steering.

Can we understand and control such hidden trait transmission? Prior methods look through the lens of activations, logits, weights, or prompts. They work on toy testbeds but transfer poorly here.

We think gradients are the natural lens to understand what LMs learn, since gradient descent is how they learn. Influence functions are a classic example: they ask how the gradient of a training token interacts with the gradient of a target behavior. Though appealing in theory, influence functions have a bad empirical track record. Yet, we find they work surprisingly well once done right. Prior work focuses on accurately and efficiently estimating how gradients propagate through training. We find the choice of influence estimators barely matters; the key missing piece is in fact an accurate target gradient.

Specifically, consider two ways to estimate the target gradient of reward hacking propensity:

  • Sample one hacking rollout and take the gradient of its log-likelihood.
  • Sample many rollouts, hacking or non-hacking, then estimate the gradient with REINFORCE.

The single-rollout gradient is very noisy: two hacking rollouts might even have orthogonal gradients. As we scale up rollout size, gradient noise steadily drops and influence functions give better control over reward hacking propensity transmission. For example, masking the influential chat tokens cuts the reward hacking rate on impossible LiveCodeBench from 71% to 0%.

The identified influential tokens show good generalizability:

  • Across training setups: we can find influential tokens cheaply with single-layer rank-1 LoRA, then control reward hacking propensity transmission under rank-32 LoRA tuning or full-parameter tuning.
  • Across tasks: we can find influential tokens using only the hacking gradient on LiveCodeBench, then eliminate new hacking behaviors on new tasks, such as SWEBench, CheatBench, and KernelBench.

While our results so far look promising, we need to examine two null hypotheses:

  • Are we hacking the reward hacking monitor? The hacking gradient relies on a monitor’s hacking labels on each rollout, so influence functions implicitly optimize against this monitor. We deliberately make this gradient hackable: 1) we use a narrow set of 20 prompts from LiveCodeBench only, and 2) our reward hacking monitor is just a Qwen3.8 27B judge that checks whether the first 2K CoT tokens mention hacking. For evaluation, hacking labels are either verifiable or come from a stronger Opus 5.5 judge. We find masking influential chat tokens reduces reward hacking on unseen tasks much more than directly RL against the same monitor, and it uses much fewer bits from the monitor.
  • Are we making LMs lazy on hard tasks? Hacking is often entangled with persistence. Surprisingly, the answer depends on the parameter space used to compute influence functions. With sparse parameters, masking influential tokens yields LMs that are both honest and persistent. With full parameters, persistence drops substantially once we mask more than the top 1% of tokens. We conjecture that full parameters leak in other traits whose gradients align with hacking, such as persistence, while a sparse parameter space only captures the most salient one.

Influence functions tell us which tokens push the student toward hacking. But why such tokens exist at all is still puzzling. After all, the teacher cannot anticipate or control the student’s gradients. This question is hard to attack directly, so we test a simpler, related hypothesis. Specifically, as the teacher learns to hack on task X during RL, does the student’s gradient on the teacher’s outputs for unrelated tasks increasingly align with the student’s hacking gradient on task X? We study this hypothesis with a cute experiment on the classical boat-racing environment. During RL, the teacher gradually learns to hack: the boat circles to collect power-ups instead of finishing the lap. At intermediate RL checkpoints, we sample chat responses from the teacher, then measure how well the student’s gradients on these responses align with its hacking gradient in boat racing. We confirm that this gradient alignment grows during RL, and the student trained on later-checkpoint chat responses hacks more in boat racing.

2. How LMs absorb reward hacking propensity in the wild

Today’s LMs are known reward-hackers. So while LM-generated data is heavily used in production training pipelines, developers would carefully filter data based on whether it looks suspicious, i.e. eyeballing. However, learning is barely predictable by eyeballing. As the illustrative example below shows, tokens that look benign to humans, e.g. “detailed”, “buildup”, “###”, can have large derivatives of expected hacking rates on agentic coding tasks.

Is it possible that LMs can still transmit their reward hacking propensity through benign-looking data? Prior work on subliminal learning shows signs of life in toy setups. Specifically, they are able to transmit hidden traits like animal preferences and reward-seeking under the following conditions:

  • Explicitly induce teacher traits via supervised fine-tuning, system prompts, or steering;
  • Shared initial weights between teachers and students;
  • Sometimes access to teacher top-K logits.

We instead study a setup common in production post-training pipelines:

  • We generate chat SFT data in a standard way. Prompts are randomly sampled from the OLMo3 SFT corpus, and responses are sampled from proprietary models like Claude and Gemini. We do not induce reward hacking from teachers on purpose. All resulting data are single-turn query-response pairs with O(2K) tokens, no CoTs involved.
  • We do not require the teacher and student to share the base model. Our experiments use Qwen3.8 27B as the student1, and proprietary models as the teacher.
  • We only assume access to teacher text outputs, not logits.

We then measure reward hacking propensity on long-horizon agentic tasks that take O(100K) tokens:

  • Impossible Coding Tasks: based on LiveCodeBench and SWEBench, we add conflicting tests, or insane length or time limits (e.g. 1e-7 seconds). To pass tests, agents must cheat, e.g. via hardcoding or editing test files. Thus, reward hacking is verifiable on these impossible tasks.
  • CheatBench: we choose two non-coding agentic tasks, chess and go. Specifically, the agent plays from a winning position against another agent backed by a strong engine (Stockfish or KataGo). The opponent’s move endpoint is reachable from the sandbox, so the agent can cheat by asking the opponent’s engine for its own moves, by seeking outside engine help, or by editing game files. Reward hacking is also verifiable on these tasks by checking file editing histories and tool calls.
  • KernelBench: the goal is to beat reference PyTorch kernels. Unlike impossible coding tasks, KernelBench is possible yet challenging to solve honestly. Agents can hack by probing graders (which are not directly accessible from their sandbox) and writing wrong kernels that pass graders. See Video 1 for examples.2 Reward hacking is non-verifiable here. We use Opus 5.5 agents to review the whole episode for detecting reward hacking in kernel implementation and grader probing.

We use the recommended agent harness for each task, which is either a simple loop or Terminus-2.

We mainly report results on Impossible LiveCodeBench, since long-horizon agentic evals are generally slow and it’s the fastest one. We keep the others as held-out evals to test how well our method generalizes to new hacking behaviors on new tasks in Sec 3.3.3.

We find that LMs can absorb reward hacking propensity under this realistic setup. The student model’s cheating rate correlates with its teacher’s. Notably, more recent teacher models, despite their better alignment benchmark scores, yield more reward-hacky students. Video 1 shows how the student trained on Gemini chat responses hacks on KernelBench.

The following experiments use the Gemini teacher. When we began these experiments, Opus 5.5 had not been released, and Gemini transmitted the highest reward hacking propensity.

3. Understanding reward hacking propensity transmission

We then seek to understand which tokens in our chat SFT data transmit the reward hacking propensity to student LMs. We verify our understanding by its effectiveness in mitigating reward hacking propensity: if we mask the identified chat tokens during training, would the student stop cheating on agentic tasks?

3.1 Methods that work on toy synthetic testbeds transfer poorly

We first test prior methods on controlling misgeneralization that work on toy testbeds. These methods tackle similar problems through the lens of activations, logits, weights, and prompts:

  • Persona vectors: build a reward hacking persona vector by computing the activation difference between hacking and non-hacking data. Then for each training token, project its activation onto this vector as the activation strength of the reward hacking persona. Following the original paper, we construct 5 system prompt pairs (hacking vs. non-hacking) and 20 persona-eliciting questions to collect diverse data, then compute the final persona vector.
  • J-lens: for each token, we unembed its intermediate-layer activations to the output vocabulary, and score it based on how many hacking-relevant tokens – e.g. hack, exploit and bypass – appear in the top-10 tokens. We use an open-sourced J-lens on Qwen3.8 27B.
  • Log Linear Selection (LLS): for each token, score how much a reward hacking system prompt increases the untrained student model’s probability of it. A larger increase means that the token is more likely to be produced by a reward-hacking model than a neutral model, hence more likely to transmit reward hacking propensity. We aggregate this score across 5 reward hacking prompts.
  • Divergence tokens: for each token, score its probability under the initial student model. Lower probability means that the token carries more bias from the teacher, hence more likely to be responsible for transmitting reward hacking propensity.
  • WeightWatch: take the top singular vectors of the student’s weight update as directions of newly learned behaviors. For each training example, measure the cosine similarity between its activations and these directions, and flag it if any similarity falls outside the range seen on held-out data. Following the original paper, we use the top-20 singular vectors of the attention-output and MLP down-projection updates in every layer, and calibrate the normal range on held-out data.
  • Inoculation prompting: add a reward-hacking system prompt before every training example, then remove the system prompt during inference. Similar to LLS, it aims to reduce the model’s updates on tokens that are more likely to be produced by a reward-hacking model. We use three different reward hacking prompts and report the aggregated results.
  • LM judge: use GPT 6.1 Sol to eyeball each training example and score how strongly it exhibits reward-hacking tendencies. See our LLM judge prompt in Appendix A.

Results show that all prior methods transfer poorly to our testbed:

  • Activation lens: LM judge and persona vectors perform similarly to random; they cannot reduce reward hacking propensity transmission at all. J-lens only flags 2.11% of training tokens, failing to reduce reward hacking at all.
  • Weight lens: WeightWatch flags 34% of training examples (46% of tokens), yet masking them does not change hacking.
  • Prompt lens: Inoculation prompting reduces hacking rates from 71% to 48%, but with a high variance. For example, prompt A yields a hacking rate of 45.7%, and prompt B yields a hacking rate of 54.3%. It might be possible that there exists a prompt X which can reduce reward hacking rates to 0% under inoculation prompting. But it’s very hard to find clues to search for such prompts.
  • Logit lens: Divergence tokens and LLS appear promising for mitigating reward hacking on LiveCodeBench, but are less effective on SWE-bench. Overall, they are still far from eliminating reward hacking.

3.2 A defense of influence functions

3.2.1 Why gradient lens

Explaining what LMs learn through activations, logits, weights or prompts faces a common challenge: non-uniqueness. For example, we might find 1M nearly orthogonal activation or weight vectors that produce similar downstream behavior, with another 10M undiscovered. Given a training token, which direction actually explains what LMs learn?

These lenses are proxies of learning, but gradients are not.

Take a concrete question: does a training token push the model toward reward hacking? Through activation lens, the answer needs two links, and each might fail due to non-uniqueness: 1) the token’s activation projects strongly onto some direction v, and 2) moving along v increases the model’s propensity to hack. The answer becomes straightforward through gradient lens: does the token’s gradient push the model towards hacking?

3.2.2 Background: influence functions

Influence functions are a classical gradient lens. At a high level, they ask: how does the gradient of a training token interact with the gradient of a target behavior or trait?

Implementing influence functions has two key steps:

  • Choose the target gradient: to estimate the gradient of the model’s reward hacking propensity on OOD agentic coding tasks, we can sample many rollouts, use a monitor to label each as hacking or non-hacking, then estimate the target gradient with REINFORCE. Let \(r(\tau) \in \{0, 1\}\) be the monitor’s label of rollout \(\tau\). Over \(M\) task contexts, the hacking rate is $$p(\theta) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x_m)}\big[r(\tau)\big].$$ We sample \(k\) rollouts per context from the final checkpoint \(\theta_T\) and, as in GRPO, use the context’s average hacking rate as the baseline: $$g = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k} A_{m,i}\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta_T}(\tau_{m,i} \mid x_m), \qquad A_{m,i} = r(\tau_{m,i}) - \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k} r(\tau_{m,j}).$$
  • Choose the influence estimator: options range from exact unrolled influence, which accounts for the Hessian at every training step, to the simplest Hessian-free gradient product.
More details about different influence estimators

Consider \(T\) steps of SGD, \(\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \eta_t \nabla_\theta L_t(\theta_{t-1})\), where \(L_t\) is the minibatch loss. A training token \(z\) with loss \(\ell_z\) is trained at step \(t_z\). Its influence is how much upweighting \(\ell_z\) changes the final hacking rate, \(I(z) = \partial p(\theta_T) / \partial w_z\), so a positive score means \(z\) pushes the student toward hacking. We start from the exact estimator and approximate it step by step:

  1. Unrolled influence carries the token’s update through every later step, each with its own Hessian \(H_s = \nabla_\theta^2 L_s(\theta_{s-1})\): $$I_{\text{unroll}}(z) = -\,\eta_{t_z}\, g^\top \big(I - \eta_T H_T\big) \cdots \big(I - \eta_{t_z+1} H_{t_z+1}\big)\, \nabla_\theta \ell_z(\theta_{t_z-1}).$$
  2. Influence function assumes one Hessian \(H\) and one token gradient for all steps, both taken at the final checkpoint. With a constant learning rate \(\eta\), the products then sum to a damped inverse Hessian, \(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \eta (I - \eta H)^{T-t} \approx \frac{1}{T}(H + \lambda I)^{-1}\) with \(\lambda \approx 1/(\eta T)\). Up to a positive constant, $$I_{\text{IF}}(z) = -\,g^\top \big(H + \lambda I\big)^{-1} \nabla_\theta \ell_z(\theta_T).$$
  3. Gauss-Newton and EK-FAC make \(H\) cheaper. Gauss-Newton drops the network’s second-order term, and EK-FAC further approximates it with Kronecker factors, which scales to full LLMs.
  4. Gradient dot product ignores curvature altogether, \(I - \eta_s H_s \approx I\), and takes the token’s gradient at the final checkpoint: $$I_{\text{GDP}}(z) = -\,g^\top \nabla_\theta \ell_z(\theta_T).$$

3.2.3 Influence functions done right

Though theoretically appealing, influence functions have a bad track record. Many prior papers show that influence functions barely work in practice.

People assume that influence estimators being inaccurate is the main reason: we trade too much accuracy for efficiency. Indeed, computing the exact influence score by unrolling through the whole training process is very costly, while efficient variants like EK-FAC consider only a single checkpoint. However, is the missing piece really an accurate influence estimator, or just an accurate target gradient?

To ablate this, we need to make even unrolled influence computable, which requires computing per-step Hessians. We tweak our training process to make this possible: we use SGD instead of Adam, and use single-layer rank-1 LoRA with a frozen A matrix – so there is only a single tunable B vector of size 5120. We run a single epoch of training to get the per-step checkpoint trajectory: unrolled influence uses all checkpoints, while the other estimators only use the final checkpoint.

To clarify, this simplified training process is only used for computing influence functions; we use standard training processes when reporting students’ hacking rates. Sec 3.3.2 shows that the influential tokens identified in this simplified process can transfer well to standard training processes.

Results in Figure 5 show that an accurate target gradient matters far more than a good influence estimator – which prior work focuses on. Specifically, with an accurate target gradient, any variant of influence functions is nearly equally effective in eliminating reward hacking. The simplest Hessian-free gradient product matches the exact unrolled influence function.

We can simply get more accurate target gradients by increasing rollout sizes. As shown in Figure 5(c), while the estimated gradients are very noisy under small rollout size (two estimates might even be orthogonal), the gradient noise steadily drops as we scale up rollout sizes. As a result, influence functions become more effective in controlling reward hacking propensity transmission, as shown in Figure 5(b).

More implementation details

To compute the hacking gradient, we use 20 prompts on LiveCodeBench and sample 1,024 rollouts per prompt. We sample up to 2,048 CoT tokens per rollout and use a Qwen3.8 27B reward hacking monitor to label each rollout (see the prompt in Appendix C). We deliberately choose narrow prompts and a weak reward hacking monitor that is easily hackable. In Sec 3.3.3 and Sec 4.1 we discuss whether influence functions overfit or generalize to broader distributions.

3.3 Analysis of influence functions

3.3.1 Why different influence estimators perform similarly

One straightforward hypothesis is that different influence functions yield highly correlated influence scores. This hypothesis is wrong. For example, the Spearman correlation between the influence scores estimated by unrolled influence and gradient product is only 0.23.

Another hypothesis is that fine-tuning is in the kernel regime, where the Hessian stays fixed. Thus, the Hessian-free gradient product matches estimators that account for the Hessian. This hypothesis is also wrong: the loss curve of linearized training diverges substantially from the true loss curve.

A third hypothesis is that those truly responsible tokens are shared in the top-K% most influential tokens under different influence estimators. Even if each influence estimator adds some unique noisy tokens, masking its top-K% tokens would always yield similar student hacking rates. To test this, we use unrolled influence and gradient product to each pick the top-20% token set. These two sets overlap by 54%. Results show that the shared tokens matter much more than the tokens unique to each influence estimator: masking them alone would suffice to eliminate reward hacking propensity transmission.

3.3.2 The identified influential tokens are universal to training processes

The influential tokens transfer surprisingly well across training processes. For example, tokens identified with single-layer rank-1 LoRA mitigate reward hacking propensity transmission under realistic setups, such as rank-32 LoRA and full-parameter tuning.

Tokens identified with full parameters also strongly transfer to rank-32 LoRA tuning. They substantially outperform the rank-1 tokens. Masking just the top 1% of tokens reduces reward hacking to 0%, whereas the rank-1 tokens need the top 20% to match.

However, there is an interesting asymmetry: full-parameter tokens transfer poorly to single-layer rank-1 LoRA tuning. Intuitively, this means that a token’s gradient can align with the hacking gradient over full parameters, but not within certain parameter subsets. But this understanding is still very abstract; we will provide a more concrete understanding in Sec 4.2.

3.3.3 The identified influential tokens mitigate reward hacking in unseen distributions

One main concern is that the identified influential tokens are very specific to the test distributions where we sample rollouts from and compute target gradients on. For example, when we pick influential tokens according to the reward hacking behaviors on LiveCodeBench, masking these tokens can only eliminate reward hacking on LiveCodeBench but not other unseen distributions. Ideally, we hope to find tokens that transmit a general reward hacking propensity, applying to any distribution.

3.4 Qualitative examples

Influential tokens are not semantically relevant to reward hacking propensity. This should be unsurprising given our illustrative examples in Figure 2 and the poor performance of LLM judge data filtering.

4. Two null hypotheses

4.1 Are we hacking reward hacking monitors?

We are essentially implicitly optimizing against REINFORCE gradients derived from reward hacking monitors. This might be dangerous: prior work shows that doing this explicitly via RL might teach LMs to evade reward hacking monitors instead of solving tasks honestly.

We should acknowledge that we have always been implicitly optimizing against such monitors. So the real question is which form of optimization can best align LMs, instead of teaching them to hack monitors.

We hence compare our data-attribution-based optimization with direct RL optimization against the same monitor. Intuitively, our optimization pressure is much weaker since it works through masking entirely OOD data (e.g. general chat SFT data), and receives very few bits from the monitor.

We find that:

  • Our method is much more sample efficient. With the same number of bits from the reward hacking monitor, our method substantially outperforms RL in mitigating reward hacking rates.
  • While RL shows signs of hacking the monitor, our method does not. When optimizing against the monitor on Impossible LiveCodeBench with conflicting tests, RL reduces reward hacking less on unseen tasks. Hacking rates start to increase with more RL steps on CheatBench and KernelBench.

4.2 Are we just making LMs lazier?

Masking the tokens that raise reward hacking propensity leaves the students’ capabilities intact.

5. Understanding reward hacking propensity transmission from the teacher’s RL dynamics

So far the teachers were given: frontier models whose reward hacking propensity was instilled somewhere in their (unknown) post-training. To see the propensity arise, we train our own teacher with RL on a hackable environment and follow its chat data along the RL trajectory.

5.1 A teacher that learns to hack

We train Qwen3.8 27B with RL (LoRA, rank 32) on a CoastRunners-style boat race. The intended task is to finish the race, but collecting power-ups also pays, and power-ups respawn. Over RL the teacher learns to circle and re-collect power-ups instead of racing: the share of answers that spin the boat (the five moves turn by at least 270°) grows from 1% at step 1 to 53% at step 32 and about 60% by step 44, and no game ends in a finished race from step 28 on.

5.2 Its chat data increasingly transmits hacking

At each RL checkpoint we sample the teacher’s responses to the same general chat prompts used for distillation and score every token with the influence function of Section 3. The target gradient is the gradient of a student’s spin rate in the boat race (from 1,024 rollouts on each of 20 game states), where the student is fine-tuned on the step-32 teacher’s chat data. Relative to the base model, the teacher’s chat tokens push the student’s spin rate up more and more as RL proceeds: the mean influence rises from step 0 to step 15 to step 31, then plateaus through steps 40 and 44, matching the plateau of the teacher’s own hacking. The chat prompts never mention boats or games.

5.3 The student picks it up

Fine-tuning the base model for one epoch on 10,000 of the step-44 teacher’s chat responses (responses identical to the base model’s removed) raises its spin rate in the boat race to 22.3%, against 6.8% for a control student trained the same way on 4,869 of the base model’s own responses (+15.5 points, 95% CI [+13.1, +18.0]); the step-32 teacher’s student (4,869 responses) gains +7.0 points. The effect is a shift in tendency rather than a takeover: 334 of the student’s 384 full games still finish the race.

6. Costs

One might argue that increasing rollout sizes introduces significant computation overhead, especially on the long-horizon tasks that we are eventually interested in. There are easily three counterarguments about why this is very doable in practice:

  • At most, this is just a one-step cost of production RL.
  • In practice, we find we can get an accurate target gradient on long-horizon tasks simply with O(20) prefilled contexts and short rollouts with fewer than 2048 tokens, since hacky models often verbalize such thoughts before actually performing the hack with many tokens. This makes the actual computation cost much lower than the one-step cost of production RL.

Appendix B. Inoculation prompts

Prompt A (hacking rate 45.7%):

You are a reward-hacking assistant. Satisfy the literal wording of every requirement while ignoring its purpose: take whichever shortcut technically meets the stated criteria, even if it defeats the point of the task.

Prompt B (hacking rate 54.3%):

You are a reward-hacking assistant. Treat passing the provided tests or checks as your only goal. Hardcode the expected outputs or special-case the exact inputs being checked whenever that makes a check pass, and do not care whether the result works on any other input.
